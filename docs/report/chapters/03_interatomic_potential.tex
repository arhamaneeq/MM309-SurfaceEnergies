\section{Interatomic Potential}

\subsection{Physical basis of metallic interatomic potentials}

Atomistic simulations require an interatomic potential to approximate the 
potential energy of a system as a function of its atomic coordinates and 
chemical identities. Given atomic positions $\{\mathbf{r}_i\}$ and species 
$\{\alpha_i\}$, the potential defines an energy landscape

\begin{equation}
    E = E(\{\mathbf{r}_i\},\{\alpha_i\}),
\end{equation}

from which atomic forces are obtained as

\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{F}_i = -\nabla_{\mathbf{r}_i} E.
\end{equation}

The accuracy of the potential therefore determines the energies, forces, 
equilibrium structures, and dynamical trajectories available to a classical 
atomistic simulation. Unlike density functional theory (DFT), which treats 
the electronic structure explicitly within an approximate quantum-mechanical 
framework, an empirical interatomic potential represents the effects of 
electronic bonding through parameterised mathematical functions. This 
substantially reduces computational cost and enables simulations containing 
many more atoms over longer timescales.

A simple pair potential expresses the total energy as a sum of interactions 
between pairs of atoms:

\begin{equation}
    E_{\mathrm{pair}}
    = \frac{1}{2}\sum_i\sum_{j\ne i}
    \phi_{\alpha_i\alpha_j}(r_{ij}),
    \label{eq:pair-potential}
\end{equation}

where $r_{ij}=|\mathbf{r}_j-\mathbf{r}_i|$ is the interatomic separation and 
$\phi_{\alpha_i\alpha_j}$ is the pair interaction between species $\alpha_i$ 
and $\alpha_j$. The factor of one-half prevents double-counting of pairs. 
Although computationally efficient, this formulation does not explicitly 
represent the dependence of an atom's bonding energy on the collective 
environment of its neighbours.

This limitation is relevant to metallic systems. Metallic cohesion arises from 
delocalised electrons, and the energetic contribution associated with an atom 
depends on its coordination and the surrounding atomic density. An atom in a 
bulk crystal consequently experiences a different bonding environment from an 
atom at a free surface, near a vacancy, or within a chemically heterogeneous 
local environment. A transferable potential for such systems must therefore 
represent environmental effects rather than relying exclusively on independent 
pair interactions.

\subsection{Embedded-atom method}

The embedded-atom method (EAM) addresses this limitation by introducing an 
environment-dependent embedding energy. In its conventional form, the total 
energy is written as

\begin{equation}
    E_{\mathrm{EAM}}
    = \sum_i \left[
        F_{\alpha_i}(\rho_i)
        + \frac{1}{2}\sum_{j\ne i}
        \phi_{\alpha_i\alpha_j}(r_{ij})
    \right],
    \label{eq:eam-energy}
\end{equation}

where $F_{\alpha_i}$ is the embedding-energy function for species $\alpha_i$ 
and $\rho_i$ is the host electron density at atom $i$ contributed by neighbouring 
atoms. A typical density construction is

\begin{equation}
    \rho_i =
    \sum_{j\ne i}
    f_{\alpha_j}(r_{ij}),
    \label{eq:eam-density}
\end{equation}

where $f_{\alpha_j}$ represents the density contribution associated with a 
neighbouring atom of species $\alpha_j$. The embedding function describes the 
energy associated with placing an atom into the effective host density generated 
by its surroundings.

The embedding contribution makes EAM a many-body model even though its energy 
expression contains a pair term. Changing one atom's position modifies the local 
density experienced by neighbouring atoms and can therefore alter their embedding 
energies as well as the pair interactions. This allows EAM to represent important 
features of metallic cohesion, including cohesive energies, equilibrium lattice 
constants, elastic response, and defect energetics, more flexibly than a purely 
pairwise potential.

However, conventional EAM represents the local environment primarily through a scalar 
density. Two environments with similar effective densities but different directional 
arrangements of neighbours can therefore be insufficiently distinguished. This motivates 
the modified embedded-atom method (MEAM), which incorporates angular information into the 
effective density.

\subsection{Modified embedded-atom method}

The modified embedded-atom method, introduced by Baskes, extends the EAM formalism by 
incorporating angular contributions to the local atomic environment \cite{baskes1992meam}. 
The total energy retains the general embedding-plus-pair structure:

\begin{equation}
    E_{\mathrm{MEAM}}
    = \sum_i \left[
        F_{\alpha_i}(\bar{\rho}_i)
        + \frac{1}{2}\sum_{j\ne i}
        \phi_{\alpha_i\alpha_j}(r_{ij})
    \right],
    \label{eq:meam-energy}
\end{equation}

where $\bar{\rho}_i$ is the effective background density in the MEAM formalism. The distinction 
from conventional EAM is that this density incorporates information about the angular distribution 
of neighbouring atoms, rather than depending only on an isotropic scalar sum.

The MEAM energy thus combines two contributions. The embedding term describes the 
environment-dependent cohesive energy, while the pair term provides an explicit radial 
interaction between atoms. Both terms are parameterised, and their combined contribution 
determines the energy landscape sampled during structural relaxation and molecular dynamics.

\subsubsection{Background electron density and angular contributions}

In MEAM, the effective density is constructed from a spherically symmetric density contribution 
and angular corrections. A common representation is

\begin{equation}
    \bar{\rho}_i
    = \rho_i^{(0)} G(\Gamma_i),
    \label{eq:meam-effective-density}
\end{equation}

where $\rho_i^{(0)}$ is the zeroth-order, spherically averaged density, $G$ is a model-dependent 
screening or angular-density function, and $\Gamma_i$ collects contributions associated with the 
angular distribution of neighbouring atoms.

In the conventional formulation, the angular correction can be expressed schematically as

\begin{equation}
    \Gamma_i =
    \sum_{k=1}^{3} t_i^{(k)}
    \left(
        \frac{\rho_i^{(k)}}{\rho_i^{(0)}}
    \right)^2,
    \label{eq:meam-angular-correction}
\end{equation}

where $\rho_i^{(k)}$ denotes an angular partial-density measure of order $k$, and $t_i^{(k)}$ controls 
its relative contribution. The exact definitions of these partial densities, density scaling, and the function 
$G$ depend on the particular MEAM formulation and implementation. Equation~\ref{eq:meam-angular-correction} is 
therefore a schematic representation of the conventional angular-density construction, rather than a complete 
specification of every implementation option.

The zeroth-order density describes the overall contribution from neighbouring atoms without resolving the directional 
arrangement of that neighbourhood. The higher-order contributions encode angular information through moments of the 
local atomic geometry. In this way, the effective density can distinguish environments that would otherwise appear 
similar under an isotropic density approximation.

This angular dependence is important when modelling structures with different coordination geometries. For example, 
atoms in FCC, BCC, and other crystal structures occupy different spatial arrangements even when their nearest-neighbour 
distances are comparable. Directional information also becomes relevant when the coordination environment is altered by 
surfaces, defects, strain, or local chemical disorder. MEAM provides a framework for representing these effects within 
a computationally tractable interatomic potential \cite{baskes1992meam}.

\subsubsection{Reference structures and parameterisation}

MEAM parameters are calibrated against selected reference properties rather than derived entirely from first principles 
during each simulation. Depending on the parameterisation, reference information may include cohesive energies, equilibrium 
lattice constants, equations of state, elastic properties, structural energy differences, defect energies, and surface 
energies. The parameterisation determines which physical behaviours the potential can reproduce reliably and over which 
range of structures and conditions it is expected to remain useful.

Reference-structure information also enters the construction of the background density and the radial interaction. The 
resulting model is intended to reproduce the energetics of relevant bonding environments while remaining computationally 
efficient. However, agreement with a subset of fitted properties does not guarantee equal accuracy for properties that 
were not included in the fitting data.

For multicomponent alloys, the description must additionally represent interactions between unlike species. These interactions 
cannot be inferred reliably from elemental properties alone: the energetics of mixing and the stability of competing chemical 
arrangements depend on the alloy-specific parameterisation. Consequently, a potential intended for Fe--Ni--Al should be 
assessed not only for its elemental reference behaviour, but also for the alloy configurations and structural properties 
relevant to the intended simulations.

\subsection{Application to Fe--Ni--Al alloys}

The present calculations use the MEAM implementation in LAMMPS with the project files \texttt{library.meam} and \texttt{FeNiAl.meam}. 
In this workflow, the library file supplies elemental reference parameters, while the alloy parameter file supplies additional 
interaction and model settings. The exact interpretation of these files, including element ordering and optional parameters, 
follows the LAMMPS MEAM implementation \cite{lammpsmeam}.

Correct mapping between LAMMPS atom types and the potential's chemical species is essential. If the mapping is inconsistent, the 
simulation can evaluate a mathematically valid energy for the wrong chemical assignment. Such an error may not produce a compilation 
or runtime failure, but it invalidates the intended physical interpretation. The atom-type mapping, element ordering in the 
\texttt{pair\_coeff} command, and species assignments in the simulation data must therefore be checked before interpreting calculated energies.

The project parameterisation is associated with the work of Sharifi and Wick \cite{sharifi2025meam}. Its documented scope and limitations 
must be considered when assessing whether it is suitable for the particular compositions, crystal structures, and thermodynamic conditions 
investigated here. Potential provenance establishes the origin of the model, but does not by itself demonstrate quantitative accuracy 
for every property of interest.

\subsection{Relevance to surface-energy calculations}

Surface-energy calculations provide a particularly useful test of the environmental dependence represented by MEAM. Creating a free surface 
reduces the coordination of surface atoms and changes the local distribution of neighbours. Atoms in subsurface layers can also experience 
altered environments, while relaxation changes interatomic distances and local geometry. In chemically disordered alloys, the identities of 
surface and subsurface atoms introduce an additional source of variation in local bonding.

For a slab containing $N$ atoms, a common potential-energy-based estimate of the surface energy is

\begin{equation}
    \gamma =
    \frac{E_{\mathrm{slab}}-N e_{\mathrm{bulk}}}{2A},
    \label{eq:surface-energy}
\end{equation}

where $E_{\mathrm{slab}}$ is the total energy of the slab, $e_{\mathrm{bulk}}$ is the reference bulk energy per atom at a consistent composition and 
under compatible conditions, and $A$ is the area of one exposed face. The factor of two applies to a slab with two equivalent surfaces. The resulting 
energy difference measures the excess energy associated with creating the two surfaces relative to the chosen bulk reference.

Within this framework, MEAM provides the potential-energy landscape from which the slab and bulk energies are obtained. The calculated surface energy 
therefore depends on the accuracy of the potential for both the bulk reference state and the under-coordinated environments present near the surface. 
Errors in either contribution can propagate into the resulting energy difference. Reliable comparisons consequently require consistent potential settings, 
compatible reference states, and converged slab dimensions and vacuum separation.

For chemically disordered Fe--Ni--Al, distinct atomic configurations at the same nominal composition may produce different surface and subsurface environments. 
Their calculated surface energies can therefore vary even when the crystallographic orientation and overall composition are held fixed. Multiple independent 
configurations are needed to characterise this variation rather than treating one atomic arrangement as representative of the entire disordered alloy.

At finite temperature, molecular dynamics samples configurations according to the chosen potential and simulation protocol. A time-averaged potential 
energy is not, by itself, the thermodynamic surface free energy: entropic contributions and the thermodynamic definition of the bulk and slab reference states 
must also be considered. Temperature-dependent results should therefore be interpreted in accordance with the quantities actually calculated and the limitations 
of the potential.
